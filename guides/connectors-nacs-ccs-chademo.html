EV connectors: NACS, CCS, CHAdeMO and the rest

9DC Editorial · Updated October 2026 · 7 min read

The plug on your car decides which chargers you can use without an adapter. Here is how the major standards differ, where each is used, and what to check before you rely on an adapter on a road trip.

The connectors at a glance

NACS (SAE J3400)Both, one compact plugNorth AmericaOriginally Tesla's design; standardised by SAE and adopted by most North American automakers for new models.

CCS2 (Combo 2)Both — Type 2 plug plus two DC pinsEurope, UK, India, Australia, much of the worldThe most common DC standard outside North America, Japan and China.
Type 2 (Mennekes)AC (single or three-phase)Europe and many other regionsCan deliver up to ~22 kW AC where three-phase supply exists.

North America: the shift to J3400

For years North America had two DC standards: Tesla's connector and CCS1 for everyone else. After Tesla opened its design and SAE published it as J3400, most automakers announced J3400 ports on new vehicles and opened access to Tesla's Supercharger network for their customers, often via an adapter for cars built with CCS1 ports. Charging networks have been adding J3400 cables alongside CCS1.

Practical result: for the next several years you will see both plugs. If your car has a CCS1 port, you can still use the large CCS1 network directly, and — where your automaker has enabled it — use J3400 stations with an approved adapter.

What adapters can and cannot do

  • They change the shape, not the electronics. The car and charger must also speak a compatible protocol and the network must allow your car to start a session.
  • Use automaker-approved or certified adapters. DC adapters carry hundreds of amps; cheap ones can overheat. Check your manufacturer's list.
  • Power can be limited. Adapters and some stall configurations cap current; expect occasional slower sessions.
  • Cable reach matters. Some stations were laid out for a port on a specific corner of the car; park accordingly and never strain the cable.

Outside North America

In Europe, the UK, India, Australia and many other markets, the norm is Type 2 for AC and CCS2 for DC, so nearly every new car and fast charger matches. China uses GB/T, and Japan continues to use CHAdeMO for DC. If you import a vehicle, confirm both its plug and its communication protocol before assuming local chargers will work.

How to check your car

  1. Look at the inlet: count the pins and note whether DC pins sit below an AC plug (CCS) or within one compact plug (J3400).
  2. Check the owner's manual for maximum DC power and supported adapters.
  3. In charging apps, filter by your connector so you only see compatible stalls.
