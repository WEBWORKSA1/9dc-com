Low-voltage DC wiring: size, drop, fuses, safety

9DC Editorial · Updated October 2026 · 8 min read

"It's only 12 volts" is how DC fires start. Low voltage means high current for the same power, and a lithium battery can deliver enormous current into a fault. Good wiring comes down to three numbers: ampacity, voltage drop and fuse rating.

Why low voltage needs thick cable

Power equals volts times amps. A 1,200 W inverter draws about 100 A at 12 V, 50 A at 24 V and 25 A at 48 V. Heat in a cable is proportional to the square of current (I²R), so halving current cuts cable heating to a quarter. That is why bigger systems move to 24 or 48 V.

1. Ampacity: will the cable overheat?

Every conductor has a maximum continuous current it can carry without exceeding its insulation temperature. Ampacity depends on conductor size, insulation rating, bundling and ambient temperature. A common practice for continuous DC loads is to size for 125% of the expected current. Engine bays, enclosed conduits and hot climates need further derating.

2. Voltage drop: will the device get enough volts?

Even a cable that is cool can waste voltage over distance. DC current travels out and back, so the round-trip length counts. Common targets:

  • ≤ 3% for most circuits, including inverter and charger cables
  • ≤ 2% for battery charging and sensitive electronics
  • up to 5% for lighting and non-critical loads

The formula our wire gauge calculator uses is: required circular mils = 2 × K × I × L ÷ allowed volts drop, where K is about 12.9 for copper and 21.2 for aluminium, I is amps and L is the one-way length in feet. It then also checks ampacity and picks the larger of the two results.

3. Fuses and breakers: protect the wire

A fuse protects the cable from overheating in a fault. Rules of thumb:

  1. Rate the fuse at or below the cable's ampacity and at or above about 125% of the normal load.
  2. Place it as close as practical to the source — the battery positive terminal — commonly within about 7 in / 18 cm.
  3. Use a fuse with an interrupt rating high enough for your battery's possible short-circuit current. Large lithium banks often need Class T fuses.
  4. Fuse every branch where the conductor gets smaller.

The ten most common mistakes

  1. No main fuse at the battery.
  2. Sizing cable for current but ignoring a long run's voltage drop.
  3. Using solid household wire in vehicles where vibration demands stranded cable.
  4. Poor crimps — use the right lug, crimper and heat-shrink.
  5. Unequal cable lengths in a parallel battery bank.
  6. Missing or undersized negative/ground conductors.
  7. Mixing AC and DC breakers — DC needs DC-rated protection.
  8. Ignoring PV string voltages, which can exceed 400 V DC in residential arrays.
  9. No disconnect for servicing.
  10. Working live — always isolate the battery first.
Professional work required: rooftop solar strings, EV chargers and anything connected to a building's mains wiring operate at dangerous voltages and are usually regulated. Use a licensed electrician and follow local code (for example the NEC in the US, CEC in Canada, BS 7671 in the UK, IS/IEC standards in India).
